A post-mortem report can help explain how a person died. It records medical findings from an examination of the body. It may also give the doctor’s opinion about the cause of death. The report does not prove every fact in a criminal case. The court must consider how it was presented. The court must also compare its findings with the other evidence. This raises an important question. Can a court use the report when the doctor who prepared it does not appear? In Bangladesh, the answer depends in part on Section 509A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898. The section creates a limited exception to the usual practice of calling the doctor to give evidence.

## **What does Section 509A allow?**

Section 509A says a post-mortem report may be used as evidence in an inquiry, trial or other proceeding under the Code if the doctor who prepared it has died or cannot give evidence. The section also applies if the doctor is outside Bangladesh. In that situation, bringing the doctor to court must involve unreasonable delay, expense or inconvenience. These conditions appear in the official text of [<u>Section 509A</u>](https://bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd/act-75/section-22058.html).

The section was added through the [<u>Code of Criminal Procedure (Second Amendment) Ordinance, 1982</u>](https://bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd/act-75/act-chapter-print-248.html). It does not say that any report can be used whenever a doctor is absent. The reason for the absence must fit one of the conditions in the section. In [<u>Death Reference No. 32 of 2017</u>](https://supremecourt.gov.bd/resources/documents/2080071_DeathReference32of2017.pdf), the Supreme Court considered the use of a post-mortem report after the doctor had died. The case shows how Section 509A may apply when the doctor cannot testify because of death.

## **The usual rule is to examine the doctor**

The usual approach is for the doctor to attend court and explain the report. This allows the court to hear how the examination was conducted. It also allows both sides to ask questions about the findings  and the doctor’s opinion. In [<u>Death Reference No. 70 of 2018</u>](https://www.supremecourt.gov.bd/resources/documents/2588016_DeathReference70of2018%28Narayangonj%29.pdf), the High Court Division explained the difference between the report and the doctor’s testimony. The court treated the doctor’s testimony as substantive evidence. The report may help the doctor recall details. It does not usually replace the doctor’s testimony. This distinction matters because a report may use medical terms that need explanation. The doctor can explain what was observed during the examination. The doctor can also describe the limits of the findings. Without that explanation, the court may have less information when weighing the opinion.

## **A doctor’s absence is not enough by itself**

A court should not accept a report only because the doctor is described as unavailable. The party relying on the report must show that Section 509A applies. In [<u>Death Reference No. 38 of 2016</u>](https://www.supremecourt.gov.bd/resources/documents/1820057_D.R.38OF2016.pdf), the court discussed the conditions for using a post-mortem report under Section 509A. The judgment states that the report cannot be used under that section if the required conditions are not met. The court should therefore examine why the doctor is absent. Has the doctor died? Is the doctor unable to give evidence? Is the doctor outside Bangladesh? If so, would bringing the doctor to court cause unreasonable delay, expense or inconvenience? The record should provide enough information to answer these questions.

The decisions also show why the court must consider the facts of each case. In [<u>Death Reference No. 70 of 2018</u>](https://www.supremecourt.gov.bd/resources/documents/2588016_DeathReference70of2018%28Narayangonj%29.pdf), the High Court Division said that a doctor’s non-examination does not automatically make a report inadmissible in every situation. The judgment still describes the doctor’s testimony as the usual form of substantive evidence. The court must look at the statutory conditions and the circumstances before it.

## **A post-mortem report is an expert opinion**

A post-mortem report can help the court understand medical findings. It does not decide who caused the death. It also does not prove guilt on its own. In [<u>State v. Md. Mostafa Sarder, 16 SCOB (2022) HCD 188</u>](https://www.supremecourt.gov.bd/resources/bulletin/16/14.%20HCD%20State%20%26%20anr%20Vs.%20Md.%20Mostafa%20Sarder%20%26%20anr%20_Bhishmadev%20Chakrabortty,%20J_.pdf), the High Court Division considered the report as an expert opinion under Section 45 of the Evidence Act. An expert opinion can assist the court. The court must still decide what weight to give it. The report may not agree with eyewitness accounts or other medical findings. The court should examine the reasons for any difference. It should consider whether the doctor explains the opinion clearly. The court should also assess whether the opinion fits the rest of the evidence.

## **The report must be considered with the rest of the case**

A post-mortem report can support other evidence. It is not always the only evidence about the death. In [<u>Death Reference No. 55 of 2010</u>](https://www.supremecourt.gov.bd/resources/documents/707134_D.R.No.55of2010.pdf), the Supreme Court considered a case where the doctor had not been examined. The judgment discusses the other evidence in the case, including eyewitness accounts and the inquest report. This illustrates a broader point. A case does not automatically fail when a post-mortem report is absent or excluded. The report’s presence does not automatically prove the case either. The court must assess the full body of evidence.

Research from Bangladesh also describes the role of autopsies in investigating unnatural deaths. A study of autopsies in Kushtia explains that post-mortem examinations can support legal investigations. The study is useful for understanding the local forensic setting. It does not determine when a report is admissible under Section 509A. ([<u>Study on autopsies of unnatural deaths in Kushtia</u>](https://kumcj.com/index.php/j/article/view/77))

## **Clear reasoning helps the court assess the report**

A useful medical report should explain what the doctor observed. It should show how those observations support the doctor’s opinion. It should also state any limits that affect the conclusion.

The [<u>UK Forensic Science Regulator’s guidance on interpretation and communication</u>](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/forensic-science-activities-interpretation-and-communication-fsr-gui-0004/forensic-science-activities-interpretation-and-communication-accessible) is not Bangladesh law. It offers a useful comparison for presenting expert evidence. The guidance calls for reasoning to be clear. It also says limitations should be explained so that others can examine the opinion. This principle helps frame practical questions for a court. Which parts of the report record direct observations? Which parts express the doctor’s opinion? Does the report explain how the doctor reached that opinion? Are there limits that may affect its value? These questions do not replace the legal test in Section 509A. They help the court assess the report after deciding whether it may be used.

## **Proper procedure protects the right to challenge evidence**

A report must be properly presented before a court can rely on it. In [<u>Death Reference No. 09 of 2018</u>](https://www.supremecourt.gov.bd/resources/documents/2473086_Death_Reference_09_2018.pdf), the High Court Division noted that the post-mortem report had not been marked as an exhibit. The court therefore could not rely on it. The other side must also have a fair chance to challenge evidence used in the case. When the doctor attends court, questions can test the findings and the reasoning. When the report is used under Section 509A, the court still needs to follow the proper procedure for receiving it.

## **Practical points for lawyers**

Lawyers relying on a post-mortem report should focus on three steps:

1.  **Explain why the doctor cannot attend.** Provide information or documents that support the reason.
    
2.  **Follow the court’s procedure.** Make sure the report is properly identified and presented as evidence.
    
3.  **Compare the report with the other evidence.** Address any differences between the medical findings and witness accounts.
    

These steps help the court decide whether Section 509A applies. They also help the court assess the weight of the report.

## **Conclusion**

Section 509A allows a court to use a post-mortem report without the doctor in specific circumstances. It is a limited exception to the usual approach of examining the doctor in court. The report is not automatic or conclusive proof. The party relying on it must show that the legal conditions apply. The court must also consider whether the report was properly presented. It should then weigh the medical opinion alongside the other evidence.
