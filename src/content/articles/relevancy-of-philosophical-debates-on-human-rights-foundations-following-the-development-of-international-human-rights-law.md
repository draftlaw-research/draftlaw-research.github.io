The philosophical debates surrounding the human rights norms have been there even before the codification or establishment of any human rights mechanism. These debates still remain highly relevant to the development and interpretation of international human rights law, despite criticisms that such debates are detached from practical realities. Now there are several reasons why they remain relevant even in an era dominated by codified international human rights law, particularly in enhancing the legitimacy and universality of human rights, guiding their interpretation, and addressing emerging challenges. This article explores the relevance of these debates in three key arguments, drawing upon foundational literature and critical reflection. It further explores these arguments in detail, demonstrating the ongoing relevance of philosophical discourse in the realm of human rights.

### **Legitimacy and Universalism of Human Rights**

The legitimacy of human rights is deeply rooted in their philosophical foundations. Different philosophical traditions offer various justifications for human rights, which can enhance their acceptance and implementation globally. The difference of opinion between these schools is very evident by the [ongoing](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40390000) [debates](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/bitstream/1893/23324/1/The%20Philosophical%20Foundations%20of%20Human%20Rights%20-%20An%20Overview%20pre-proof.pdf) among them.  For instance, natural law theory suggests that human rights are inherent and universal, derived from human nature itself. This perspective argues that human rights are not granted by governments but are intrinsic to every individual, thus providing a robust [moral foundation for their legitimacy](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40390000).  By offering multiple justifications, philosophical debates help to reinforce the legitimacy of human rights from different angles, making them more compelling and universally accepted.

Furthermore, one of the core elements of this moral authority is the universality of these rights, which is another critical issue addressed by the same philosophical debates. Yet the universality of these rights even in recent times are subjected to certain criticisms and limitations. One of those criticisms are that these rights are a Western construct, but when we go through philosophical discussions, such as Jeremy Waldron’s exploration of dignity as a foundational concept, it illustrates how inherent human worth underpins the universality of rights. By framing dignity as an inalienable attribute of all individuals, Waldron aligns with the natural law tradition, which asserts that human rights exist independently of political recognition.  This perspective reinforces the moral legitimacy of rights enshrined in the UDHR, [particularly against](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199688623.003.0006) critiques that dismiss them as Western constructs. 

Another core criticism is that while international human rights law aims to apply universally, cultural relativism poses a significant challenge. But philosophical discussions can bridge the gap between universal principles and cultural specificities. For example, the concept of "[overlapping consensus](https://philarchive.org/archive/HEROCV)" suggests that different cultural and philosophical traditions can agree on certain fundamental rights, even if their underlying justifications differ.  This approach helps to reconcile the universality of human rights with respect for cultural diversity.

Additionally, Marie-Bénédicte Dembour’s work on the “Four Schools of Thought” further highlights how universal human rights can be justified through diverse philosophical traditions. For instance, while the natural school sees rights as given, the protest school emphasizes their universality through collective struggles. These perspectives [demonstrate](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40390000) that human rights are not monolithic but rather pluralistic, capable of resonating across cultural and historical contexts. 

### **Interpretative Frameworks in International Law**

Philosophical debates are essential for interpreting and applying the often-ambiguous provisions of international human rights law. Treaties like the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) articulate broad rights but leave significant room for interpretation. Theoretical frameworks help clarify these ambiguities, particularly when rights and duties conflict or evolve in scope.

For example Isaiah Berlin’s distinction between negative and positive liberty [suggests](https://cactus.utahtech.edu/green/B_Readings/I_Berlin%20Two%20Concpets%20of%20Liberty.pdf) that it contains a great interpretive value as it offers a lens to understand the dual obligations embedded in human rights law.  Negative liberty, [the right to non-interference](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights#article-9), aligns with state duties to respect individual freedoms, such as the prohibition against arbitrary detention.  Positive liberty, which involves enabling individuals to realize their potential, informs state obligations to fulfill rights, such as the [right to work](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-economic-social-and-cultural-rights#article-6).  Even Jeremy Waldron’s [reflections](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199688623.003.0006) on the pragmatic use of dignity in legal interpretation further illustrate this point. [General Comment No. 36](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/Documents/HRBodies/CCPR/CCPR_C_GC_36.pdf) by the UN Human Rights Committee, for instance, invokes dignity to interpret the right to life under Article 6 of the ICCPR, emphasizing both negative and positive obligations.  

Another phenomenon that follows is that the Judges and lawmakers frequently draw on philosophical arguments to interpret and apply human rights in various contexts. One notable case is where the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) utilized philosophical concepts to interpret the European Convention on Human Rights is [Selmouni v. France](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/fre#{%22itemid%22:[%22001-58287%22]}). Here the Court applied the doctrine of evolutive interpretation, which is grounded in the idea that the Convention is a living instrument that must be interpreted in light of present-day conditions.  This approach reflects a philosophical understanding that human rights standards evolve over time to meet contemporary societal needs.

### **Addressing Emerging Challenges in Human Rights**

Human rights in its essence are not static; they evolve in response to new challenges and societal changes. Philosophical debates play a crucial role in addressing these emerging challenges by questioning existing norms and proposing new rights. For example, [the recognition of socio-economic rights](https://doi.org/10.1080/19962126.2005.11865126), such as the right to health or education, has been influenced by philosophical arguments about what constitutes a dignified human life.  As the world confronts challenges like digital privacy, climate justice, and statelessness, foundational debates provide the conceptual tools necessary for integrating these issues into existing frameworks.

In the contemporary world, issues such as digital privacy, climate change, and artificial intelligence pose new challenges for human rights. Philosophical debates can provide the ethical frameworks needed to address these issues. For example, discussions about the right to privacy in the digital age can draw on philosophical theories of autonomy and consent. Similarly, the ethical implications of artificial intelligence can be explored through the lens of human rights, ensuring that technological advancements do not undermine fundamental rights.

Rowan Cruft has highlighted the importance of philosophical engagement in addressing these issues in his writing. [For instance](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/bitstream/1893/23324/1/The%20Philosophical%20Foundations%20of%20Human%20Rights%20-%20An%20Overview%20pre-proof.pdf), he points it out that debates about the ethical implications of artificial intelligence or the collective rights of indigenous peoples require a grounding in foundational principles to ensure coherence with existing human rights norms.  It is a rightful concern that, without such philosophical support, the expansion of rights risks fragmentation and confusion.

### **Conclusion**

In conclusion, even though international human rights law has achieved significant progress in codifying and enforcing rights, but its moral authority, implementation and adaptability is heavily dependent on ongoing philosophical engagement. Because without engaging with these philosophical foundations, international human rights law risks being seen as Eurocentric, undermining its claim to universality. Additionally, these philosophical debates enrich the practical implementation of human rights law, ensuring that legal norms remain responsive to evolving societal needs.
