Maritime commerce does not pause for procedure. In an effort to keep up with the pace of international arbitration, the Singapore International Arbitration Centre ("SIAC") introduced [emergency arbitrator provisions](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/arbitration-blog/siac-rules-2025-breaking-new-ground-in-emergency-arbitration-with-protective-preliminary-orders/?shem=aimgspe,) to its rules in 2010. Emergency arbitration ("EA") has since been adopted by most of the major arbitration institutions around the world, [including](https://foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/publications/alerts-and-updates/2025/march/key-changes-in-the-2025-siac-arbitration-rul/?shem=aimgspe,) the International Chamber of Commerce ("ICC") and the London Court of International Arbitration ("LCIA") in one form or another. Maritime claims have been at the forefront of EA. Some claims cannot wait weeks or months, as was historically the case, for an arbitral tribunal to be formed before interim measures can be sought. For example, a vessel's wrongful diversion to another port, a wrongfully imposed lien over perishable goods, or a charterer's wrongful trading in breach of charterparty terms can result in losses that mount by the hour. Time, tides and trade opportunities do not wait; maritime commercial endeavours exist within tight deadlines. Though arbitration is widely recognized and arbitral awards are enforceable in nearly every country in the world under the [New York Convention](https://www.newyorkconvention.org/english), the lack of EA would have made arbitration impractical for resolving these kinds of issues. EA rules [permit](https://jusmundi.com/en/document/publication/en-emergency-arbitrator-relief-a-practical-guide) a party to seek interim relief from an emergency arbitrator before the constitution of a full arbitral tribunal. SIAC was the first Asia-based arbitration institution to introduce an emergency arbitrator into its rules. For a maritime lawyer, the appeal is immediate: an emergency arbitrator can typically be appointed within a day and is required to render a decision within a matter of days, a timeline that maps far more naturally onto shipping's operational rhythm than the months a fully constituted tribunal would take to convene.

The practical utility of emergency arbitration in shipping disputes is best understood against the backdrop of the remedies already familiar to admiralty practice. Ship arrest remains the shipping bar's default tool for obtaining security, precisely because arbitral awards, unlike court judgments, cannot themselves compel a vessel's detention; a claimant must still turn to the courts of the state where the ship happens to be found to arrest it in rem. Emergency arbitration does not replace that remedy, but it supplies something arrest cannot: a reasoned, binding, and immediately actionable determination of the urgent relief itself. Where a charterer disputes an owner's right to withdraw a vessel for non-payment of hire, where a shipper contests a demurrage claim that is holding cargo hostage at a discharge port, or where a claim for wrongful arrest arises out of a charterparty dispute and the arbitral tribunal has not yet been constituted, an emergency arbitrator can grant or refuse an injunction, order continued performance, or direct the release of security. Once constituted, the tribunal can confirm, modify or discharge the relief granted by the emergency arbitrator.

However, over the last decade, recognition of an emergency arbitrator’s orders has taken a [stronger footing in India](https://www.wongpartnership.com/upload/medias/KnowledgeInsight/document/19095/CaseWatch_IndianSupremeCourtRecognisesEmergencyArbitrationUnderSIACRules.PDF?shem=aimgspe,). In the case of [Amazon.com NV Investment Holdings LLC v. Future Retail Ltd. & Ors.](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/104517457/), (2022) 1 SCC 209 (decided 6 August 2021), the Supreme Court of India held that an emergency arbitrator’s award passed under the applicable institutional rules, for instance SIAC’s Rules, in an arbitration seated in India amounts to an order passed by the tribunal and is enforceable under Section 17 of the [Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1306164/). In doing so, it rejected the argument that emergency arbitration is alien to Indian arbitral law. The significance of the case goes beyond the parties to it. For any party to an Indian-seated arbitration, the decision removes the uncertainty around the power of Indian courts to enforce emergency arbitrator orders under Section 17, which had given rise to hesitancy in relying on them in a time-sensitive dispute. It does not, however, resolve the enforcement of emergency orders made in foreign-seated arbitrations.

The most recent evolution, and the one most directly relevant to maritime practice, is SIAC's introduction of the Protective Preliminary Order (PPO) procedure under Rule 12.1 and Schedule 1 of the SIAC Rules 2025, which for the first time [permits a party to seek ex parte interim relief](https://www.klgates.com/thought-leadership/7th-Edition-of-the-SIAC-Rules-Defining-the-Future-of-SIAC-Arbitration-1-23-2025?shem=aimgspe,) before the counterparty is even notified, provided that party is given an opportunity to be heard at the earliest practicable time thereafter (Schedule 1, paras 25 to 29). This is precisely the scenario a shipping lawyer recognizes instinctively: a vessel about to sail out of a jurisdiction's reach, a bill of lading about to be presented to a bank, or cargo about to be released to a buyer in breach of a letter of indemnity. Notice to the other side, in such circumstances, can itself defeat the relief sought. The PPO procedure attempts to [reconcile this operational reality](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/arbitration-blog/balancing-natural-justice-and-urgency-in-ex-parte-emergency-interim-relief-the-siac-rules-2025-as-a-blueprint/?shem=aimgspe,) with the audi alteram partem principle that arbitration, unlike a without-notice court injunction, has traditionally been reluctant to compromise.

The mechanism is not without friction. First, enforceability outside the seat remains uncertain: because an emergency arbitrator's decision is typically styled an "order" rather than an "award," its status under the New York Convention, which speaks of the recognition and enforcement of arbitral awards, is contested in jurisdictions that have not, unlike India for Indian-seated arbitrations, adjudicated the point. Second, the cost of engaging an emergency arbitrator at short notice, often within twenty-four hours of appointment, can be disproportionate for the small and mid-sized charterers and shipowners who form the backbone of the tramp trade. Third, there remains an unresolved tension between an emergency arbitrator's power to order relief and a party's parallel right to arrest a vessel through the courts, since the two remedies proceed on different legal footings and before different decisionmakers, raising the practical risk of inconsistent outcomes.

None of this displaces the underlying case for emergency arbitration in maritime disputes. Shipping contracts are drafted, performed, and breached against timelines measured in laytime and notices of readiness, not litigation calendars. A dispute resolution mechanism that cannot respond within that timeframe is, for many maritime claims, no remedy at all. Emergency arbitration is especially helpful to parties in the shipping industry because interim relief can be sought and granted on a timescale commensurate with the pace of maritime commerce, and some courts, notably in India, have recognized decisions made through emergency arbitration. That position is settled for Indian-seated arbitrations under Section 17; elsewhere, the enforceability of emergency arbitrator decisions remains uncertain and should not be assumed.

Emergency arbitration is an option that becomes binding and enforceable with the right drafting and choice of seat. As trade-related disputes increasingly avoid admiralty courts in favor of arbitration, maritime counsel should consider the merits of emergency arbitration. Often an afterthought in the context of arbitration clauses, a well-drafted emergency arbitrator clause may be a party’s only recourse to protect its rights effectively in some disputes before a ship sails.
