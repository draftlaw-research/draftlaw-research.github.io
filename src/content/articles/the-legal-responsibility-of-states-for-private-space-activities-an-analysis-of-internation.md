## Introduction

Outer space is no longer explored only by governmental agencies. Private companies operate satellite networks, provide launch services, and develop commercial space technologies. These activities raise a central legal question:

> to what extent are States responsible when private entities conduct space operations, especially where several States are involved?

The [Outer Space Treaty of 1967](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/introouterspacetreaty.html) establishes the core principles governing space activities. The [1972 Liability Convention](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/introliability-convention.html) further establishes rules concerning compensation for damage caused by space objects. This article argues that these instruments remain essential, but their implementation must adapt to increasingly complex commercial missions.

## State Responsibility and National Supervision

Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty provides that States bear international responsibility for national activities in outer space, whether conducted by governmental agencies or non-governmental entities. States must ensure that such activities comply with the Treaty. Non-governmental activities require authorization and continuing supervision by the appropriate State.

These obligations mean that privatization does not remove a space activity from the international legal framework. States must establish domestic procedures to authorize operators and supervise their activities over time. Article VI, however, does not prescribe a detailed licensing system, leaving States discretion in designing national regulation. Differences between domestic systems may therefore produce uneven levels of oversight.

State responsibility under Article VI should be distinguished from liability for damage under Article VII of the Treaty and the Liability Convention. Article VI concerns compliance with international obligations; the liability framework concerns compensation for damage caused by space objects. The [Liability Convention](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/introliability-convention.html) elaborates the principle set out in Article VII.

## Identifying Launching States in Multinational Missions

Article I of the Liability Convention defines a “launching State” as a State that launches or procures the launching of a space object, or a State from whose territory or facility it is launched. Accordingly, more than one State may qualify.

A commercial mission might involve a company incorporated in one country, a satellite manufactured in another, and a launch conducted from a third State. The relevant inquiry is not simply who owns the satellite. It is whether a State meets one of the Convention’s launching-State criteria. Manufacturing a satellite or supplying technology does not, by itself, automatically make a State a launching State.

Article V addresses joint launches. Where two or more States jointly launch a space object, they are jointly and severally liable for damage caused by it. A State from whose territory or facility the object is launched is also treated as a participant in a joint launch under the Convention.

Joint and several liability allows an injured State to seek the full compensation due from any or all of the responsible launching States. States may later allocate the financial burden among themselves. A State that has paid compensation may seek indemnification from other participants, and the participants may agree on how to distribute costs. Such agreements, however, cannot prejudice the injured State’s right to claim full compensation from any or all of the liable States. This separates the victim’s right to compensation from disputes over the allocation of costs between States.

The rule is especially important where several States support a commercial mission but have different levels of operational involvement. International liability does not necessarily depend on which participant exercised the greatest technical control.

## Liability Standards and Claims

The Liability Convention applies different standards according to where damage occurs. Under Article II, a launching State is absolutely liable for damage caused by its space object on the Earth’s surface or to aircraft in flight. Under Article III, damage occurring elsewhere than on Earth’s surface generally gives rise to liability only where the damage is due to the fault of a launching State or persons for whom it is responsible.

Article IV addresses certain cases in which a space object causes damage to a third State, or to its persons or property, and provides for joint and several liability subject to its conditions. Identifying a launching State is therefore a distinct question from determining the applicable liability standard.

The Convention principally establishes a process for claims between States and certain international organizations; it does not create a general direct international claim for individuals or private companies. Article XI preserves the possibility of pursuing claims before the courts or administrative tribunals of a launching State, subject to the Convention’s rules concerning claims for the same damage.

## Regulatory Challenges and Reform

Commercial space activity creates practical challenges for national supervision. Satellite constellations, reusable launch vehicles, and multinational contracts can make monitoring and identifying responsible States more difficult. Different national licensing rules may also lead to inconsistent safety and financial-protection requirements.

States should therefore maintain effective authorization and continuing supervision, rather than treating licensing as a one-time procedure. For joint missions, participating States should clarify insurance, information-sharing, indemnification, and the internal allocation of costs. These arrangements should not diminish the rights of injured States under the Liability Convention.

The [United Nations Guidelines for the Long-term Sustainability of Outer Space Activities](https://www.unoosa.org/documents/pdf/PromotingSpaceSustainability/Publication-_Final_English_version.pdf) support responsible space operations. Guideline A.1 encourages States to adopt, revise, or amend national regulatory frameworks, taking account of their obligations as States responsible for national space activities and as launching States. The Guidelines are not binding like a treaty, but they offer practical guidance for improving national regulation and cooperation.

## Conclusion

International space law places States at the center of accountability for space activities, including those conducted by private entities. Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty requires authorization and continuing supervision, while the Liability Convention identifies launching States and establishes compensation rules for damage caused by space objects.

The Convention’s joint and several liability rules are particularly important when multiple States participate in a launch: they protect injured States while allowing participating States to settle the allocation of costs among themselves. Nevertheless, increasingly complex commercial operations call for effective national supervision, transparent agreements between launching States, and stronger international cooperation.

Commercial innovation in outer space should develop alongside legal accountability, safety, and respect for international obligations.
