# **How to Find and Use Sources**

Good research has three parts: the right source, the right weight, and the right pinpoint (the exact paragraph or page). Judges do not count your sources. They test them.

### Step 1: Learn the background first

Before you search for cases, read one good textbook chapter on the topic. This gives you the map. Without a map, you will not know which sources matter.


|  |  |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Field** | **Good starting books (use whichever your library has)** |
| Public international law (Jessup) | Shaw, *International Law*; Crawford, *Brownlie’s Principles of Public International Law*; Dixon, *Textbook on International Law* |
| IHL (Henry Dunant) | Sassòli, *International Humanitarian Law*; Solis, *The Law of Armed Conflict*; ICRC Commentaries |
| Environmental law (Stetson) | Sands, *Principles of International Environmental Law*; Birnie, Boyle and Redgwell |
| Arbitration and CISG (Vis) | Redfern and Hunter on International Arbitration; Schlechtriem and Schwenzer, *Commentary on the CISG* |


A textbook is for *learning*. Do not use it as your main authority in the memorial unless a treaty, case or official text says the same thing.

### Step 2: Know the ladder of authority

Judges ask: “How strong is your source?” Use this ladder.


|  |  |  |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Level** | **What it is** | **Examples** |
| Strongest | Treaties binding on the parties; clear customary law | Geneva Conventions; UN Charter; the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) rules that reflect custom |
| Very strong | Decisions of the main court on the point | ICJ judgments and advisory opinions; in Vis, decisions on the CISG from national courts and tribunals |
| Strong | Official codification and other tribunals | ILC Articles on State Responsibility (2001) with commentary; ITLOS, ICC, ICTY, ICTR, arbitral awards |
| Helpful | Respected scholars and expert bodies | ICRC Customary IHL Study; CISG Advisory Council Opinions; leading journal articles |
| Weak | Soft law, resolutions, press reports, blogs | UN General Assembly resolutions (unless they show custom); news; blog posts |


*For ICJ-style moots, Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute is your checklist: treaties, custom, general principles, then judicial decisions and scholars as subsidiary means.*

**Formula for weight:** Is it Binding? Is it Relevant? Is it Recent? Is it Authoritative? If a source fails any two of these four, find a better one.

### Step 3: Where to look (many are free)

Many undergrad students cannot access paid databases. You do not need them to do excellent research. Start here:

- **ICJ website.** Judgments, advisory opinions, pleadings and separate opinions, free.
- **UN Treaty Collection.** Treaty texts and status (who is a party).
- **UN International Law Commission (ILC) website.** Draft articles and commentaries.
- **ICRC IHL Databases.** Treaties, commentaries and the customary law study.
- **ITLOS, PCA and ICC websites.** Decisions, awards and legal texts.
- **UNCITRAL and CISG databases** (for example, the Pace Law CISG Database and CISG-online). Free case law and commentary on the CISG.
- **Italaw and UNCTAD Investment Policy Hub.** Investment arbitration awards and treaties.
- **Google Scholar, SSRN and open-access journals.** The European Journal of International Law and others have free articles. EJIL:Talk!, Opinio Juris, Just Security, DraftLaw Research and Völkerrechtsblog are useful for current debates, but treat them as weak sources.
- **Your university library.** Ask your librarian and your coach about JSTOR, HeinOnline, Westlaw, LexisNexis and Oxford Public International Law. Many universities have them and students never ask.
- **The snowball trick.** Take one good case or article. Read its footnotes. Find the three most useful sources there. Read their footnotes. Within an hour you have a reading list.

### Step 4: How to read a case fast and well

- Read the summary or first paragraphs to learn the question and the result.
- Find the *reasoning* paragraphs (often called “the Court’s reasoning” or “considerations”).
- Note the paragraph numbers. You will need them.
- Separate the **ratio** (the reasoning needed for the decision) from **obiter** (side comments). Judges care.
- Read separate and dissenting opinions. They tell you the other side’s best argument.
- Check what later cases said about it. Has it been followed, limited or ignored?
- Never cite a headnote or a summary website as if it were the judgment. Cite the judgment.

### Step 5: Keep a source log from day one

An example log:


|  |  |  |  |  |  |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | --------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Source** | **Pinpoint** | **What it proves (one sentence)** | **Strength** | **Issue** | **Short quote (under 25 words)** |
| Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (ICJ 1997) | para 140 | Sustainable development must guide use of shared rivers | Level 2 | Issue 2 | “…” |
| ILC Articles on State Responsibility, Art 2 | Art 2 | Elements of an internationally wrongful act | Level 3 | Issue 2 | “…” |


This log saves weeks. It also helps you find a quote in seconds when a judge asks.

## **Important tips and tricks**

### Rules for honest and safe research

- **Open every source you cite.** Never cite a case you only saw named in a blog.
- **Do not trust AI tools for citations.** Chatbots can invent cases, paragraph numbers and quotes that look real. Use them to understand a topic if you like but check every source yourself on the official site.
- **Always give a pinpoint.** A citation without a paragraph or page number looks lazy.
- **Check the rules.** Some moots restrict sources, set a cut-off date, or limit how many you can cite.
- **Quote small.** Use short quotes only where the exact words matter. Put the rest in your own words.
- **Find the other side’s sources too.** You will have to argue against them.

### Quick quality check for each source

- Is it a primary source (the actual treaty or judgment) rather than somebody’s summary?
- Does it really say what I claim? Reread the paragraph.
- Is it binding on this court, or only persuasive?
- Could the other side say it is about a different situation? How will I answer that?
