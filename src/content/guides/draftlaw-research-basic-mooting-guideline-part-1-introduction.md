# **Introduction**

A moot court is a simulated court hearing where law students argue a fictional case as if they were real advocates. There is no real client, no real judge, and no real consequence. The point is to practice the skill that law school rarely teaches directly: standing up and persuading a bench of judges with a legal argument, under pressure, out loud.

This guide is written for a student who may be joining a moot team for the first time, may not have access to expensive databases, and may be balancing classes, exams, travel costs and other team responsibilities. The language used in this guide is deliberately kept simple.

## **What are the benefits of moot?**

Mooting offers benefits that classroom learning rarely provides. It sharpens your legal research and writing by forcing you to build a persuasive, well-constructed argument from scratch, and it trains you to speak clearly and confidently under pressure, especially when judges interrupt with difficult questions. Because you argue both sides over a competition, you learn to see the strengths and weaknesses of every position, which deepens your legal reasoning and your ability to anticipate counterarguments. It also builds teamwork, time management, and professionalism, since you work closely with teammates against tight deadlines and learn the etiquette of the courtroom. Beyond skills, mooting gives you the chance to network with practitioners and academics who judge the rounds, and it strengthens your CV for careers in litigation, academia, or policy work. Above all, it turns abstract legal doctrine into something you can actually use, which makes the law far easier to understand and remember.

## **How to use this guide?**

Every competition has its own rules, and rules change every year. Treat this guide as your training manual and the official rules of the competition you are participating in as your law. If there is ever any contradiction between them, the official rules always win. This guide teaches a method; it does not replace the current rulebook.

This guide shall be treated only as a recommendation and not something written in stone, you can diverge from it, oppose it or even discard it if you can think of a better alternative. Always remember that there is no one way to moot, everyone has their own process, and this guide is a mere compilation of the process I picked up from my mooting experience both in Bangladesh and Sweden.

Another important disclaimer to keep in your mind while using the process mentioned in this guide is you will likely not do all of these things mentioned here perfectly on your first attempt, and this is perfectly normal and expected. In fact, one of the most difficult parts of mooting can be balancing all the different things you have to do and think about at the same time, and this skill will generally only develop with practice.

## **What are the core differences between moot, mock trial and debate?**

The core differences between these three forms of competition have been illustrated through the following table:


|  |  |  |  |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| **Subject** | **Moot** | **Mock Trial** | **Debate** |
| Focus | Points of law | Facts and witnesses | Opinions and policy |
| Platform | Usually, an appeal or trial before an international court | Trial court | No court |
| Judge’s Role | Asks hard legal and factual questions | Rules on evidence | Scores style and logic |
| Written Part | Yes (memorial) | Sometimes | No |
| Your Job | Persuade on the law using authorities | Examine witnesses | Persuade the audience |


## **What are the keywords you must know?**

### The Case and Its Documents

- **Moot Problem / Compromis / Fact Sheet / Agreed Facts**: The fictional record of events. (“Compromis” is the term used in international law moots such as the Jessup, where it mimics a special agreement between states.)
- **Memorial / Memorandum / Brief / Skeleton Argument**: Your written argument. Different moots use different names.
- **Issues / Questions Presented**: The specific legal questions the problem asks you to argue.
- **Clarifications**: Official answers from the organizers to questions about the moot problem. Treat these as part of the facts.
- **Rules of the Competition**: The document setting out word limits, time limits, formatting, scoring, and conduct. Read it first; breaches can cost marks.
- **Bundle / Compendium**: A short pack of authorities or documents some moots let you hand to judges.
- **Authorities**: The sources you rely on, such as cases, treaties, statutes, and scholarly writing.
- **Binding vs Persuasive Authority**: A binding authority must be followed by the court; a persuasive one may influence it but need not be followed.

### The Parties and People

- **Applicant / Claimant / Appellant / Petitioner**: The side that brings the case.
- **Respondent / Defendant**: The side that defends.
- **Counsel / Advocate**: You, in your role as the lawyer arguing the case.
- **Oralist / Speaker**: A team member who speaks in the oral rounds.
- **Bench**: The panel of judges (often three).
- **Presiding Judge / President of the Court**: The judge who chairs the panel, often the one who opens and closes the round.
- **Bailiff / Court Clerk / Timekeeper**: The person who announces the round and keeps time.
- **My Learned Friend**: The polite way to refer to your opponent.

### Courtroom Language and Etiquette

- **“May it please the Court”**: The traditional opening line before you begin.
- **“My Lord / My Lady / Your Honor / Madam President / Mr. President”**: Forms of address for judges, depending on the tradition of the moot.
- **“I appear on behalf of...”**: How you introduce yourself and the side you represent.
- **Roadmap**: A short preview of the structure of your argument, given at the start.
- **Submission**: An argument or point you put forward to the court. (“My first submission is that...”)
- **Prayer for Relief / Remedy Sought**: The exact order you ask the court to make at the end.
- **Rebuttal**: A short reply to your opponent’s arguments, if the moot allows it.
- **Sur-rebuttal**: A brief response to the rebuttal, allowed in some formats.
- **Concession**: Admitting a point is weak or accepting it for argument’s sake, which builds credibility when done strategically.
- **Without prejudice to**: Preserving your main position while engaging with a fallback point.

### Legal Argument Terms

- **Jurisdiction**: Whether the court has the power to hear the case at all.
- **Admissibility**: Whether the claim is in a proper form to be heard (for example, whether local remedies have been exhausted).
- **Standing / Locus Standi**: Whether a party has the right to bring the claim.
- **Merits**: The substance of the dispute, as opposed to preliminary questions.
- **Burden of Proof**: Which side must prove a point.
- **Standard of Proof**: How convincing the proof must be.
- **Ratio Decidendi**: The binding reasoning behind a court’s decision.
- **Obiter Dicta**: Remarks made by a judge in passing, which are not binding.
- **Precedent**: A past decision used to support an argument.
- **Distinguish**: To argue that a case relied on by the other side differs from yours on its facts or law, so it should not apply.
- **Analogy**: Arguing that a principle from one situation should apply to a similar one.
- **IRAC / CREAC**: Common argument structures: Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion (CREAC adds an Explanation of the rule).
- **Prima Facie**: On first appearance; enough evidence to establish a case unless rebutted.
- **In the Alternative**: A fallback argument you make if the court rejects your main one.

### Sources of Law (Especially in International Moots)

- **Treaty / Convention**: A written agreement between states.
- **Customary International Law**: Rules arising from consistent state practice that states follow out of a sense of legal obligation.
- **General Principles of Law**: Principles common to major legal systems.
- **Judicial Decisions**: Rulings of courts and tribunals, used as authority.
- **Teachings of Publicists**: Writings of leading scholars, used as secondary support.
- **Jus Cogens**: Fundamental norms from which no derogation is allowed.
- **Obligations Erga Omnes**: Obligations owed to the whole international community.
