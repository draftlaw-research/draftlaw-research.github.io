# **How to Handle Judges’ Questions**

Questions are the heart of a moot. They are not an attack. They are the judges’ way to test whether you really understand your case. Strong speakers’ welcome questions and use them to make their best points.

## **Why judges ask questions**

- To **clarify** something that was unclear.
- To **test** the weakest part of your argument.
- To **explore** what happens in a different situation (a “what if…?”).
- To **check** the facts or a source.
- To **help** you with a better argument.
- To **move you on** when you are repeating yourself.

When a judge interrupts, they are giving you free attention. Use it.

## **The five-step answer**

1. **LISTEN**   : Let the judge finish. Never interrupt.
2. **PAUSE**    : One or two seconds. Think about the real concern.
3. **ANSWER**   : Give the direct answer first (yes, no, or the main point).
4. **BACK IT UP**: One reason, one source or one fact.
5. **BRIDGE**   : Link back to your argument and continue.

**Never** start with a long story. Start with the answer. The judge should hear “Yes, because…” or “No, because…” within five seconds.

## **Understand the question**


|  |  |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Your situation** | **What to do** |
| You did not hear the question | “I regret I did not catch that. Could the Court please repeat the question?” |
| You heard the words but not the meaning | “Could the Court kindly rephrase the question?” |
| You think you know the concern but are not sure | Say it back: “If I understand the Court’s concern, it is whether… ” Then pause one second, and answer. If the judge corrects you, adjust |
| Two meanings are possible | “Is the Court asking about X or about Y?” |


Saying the question back also buys you a few seconds to think, and it shows you were listening.

## **Four ways to structure an answer**

**1. A single direct answer.** If the answer is simple, give it and stop.

> *Judge: “Is an email a written notice?”* 
>
> *You: “Not in these circumstances, Madam President. Article 12 requires notice to the other party, and this email went to one official, not to the State.”*

**2. A multi-point answer.** State the conclusion, then the reasons.

> *“It is not, for three reasons. First, … Second, … Third, …”*

**3. A main point and an alternative.** Defend your main position, then add a fallback.

> *“The treaty is still in force because notice was invalid. In the alternative, even if the Court finds the email valid, it came late, so the treaty is still renewed.”*

**4. A direct answer and a supplementary point.** When your answer is not strong, still answer first. Then add a point that redirects the concern.

> *“It is correct that Article 4 does not mention emergencies. But the Court’s concern is answered by Article 9, which provides a special procedure for dry-season shortages, and Ulka did not use it.”*

Do not dodge. If you avoid the question, the judge will ask it again.

## **Common question types and how to answer them**


|  |  |  |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Type** | **Example** | **How to answer** |
| Clarifying | “Is your argument that the treaty is void, or just that it has not ended?” | Answer exactly. “The Applicant’s case is that it has not ended.” |
| Fact check | “Where do the facts say Ulka held back the flow?” | Give the paragraph number. “Paragraph 9, my Lord.” |
| Source check | “What is your best authority for that?” | Name the case or treaty, pinpoint, one sentence on what it says |
| Testing the weak point | “Article 12 does not mention diplomatic channels. Why should the Court add that requirement?” | Concede what is true, then explain. “It does not, but the Court reads treaty words in context and in the light of their purpose.” |
| Hypothetical | “If Ulka had sent registered post two weeks late, would you still win?” | Accept the new facts. Apply your rule. Show your rule gives a sensible result |
| Policy | “If we accept your view, will States ever be able to end river treaties?” | Show your rule is balanced. “Yes, by giving clear notice in time.” |
| Relief | “What exactly do you ask the Court to order?” | State the order in one sentence. Know your prayer by heart |
| Curveball | A question on a topic you did not prepare | Use first principles and your theory of the case. Try an answer. Do not freeze |


### Answering hypotheticals

Do not say, “That is not our case.” The judge knows. The question tests whether your rule works in other situations.

**Method:**

```
Accept the change → apply your test → give the result → show it still fits your theory.
```

> *Judge: “Suppose Ulka had told Torsa’s ambassador in person, but not in writing.”*
>
> *You: “Then the written-notice requirement in Article 12 would still not be met. The parties chose a written form to avoid disputes about what was said. Our test is the same: a clear, formal, written notice to the State.”*

If the hypothetical shows a flaw in your argument, you may adjust it slightly (“The Applicant’s position is better put as…”). It is better to adapt than to defend something that now looks silly.

### When you do not know the answer

- **Try.** Give your best reasoning from the law and the facts. Judges give some credit for effort.
- If you really cannot answer, say clearly: “I regret I am unable to assist the Court on that point.”
- **Do not bluff.** Do not invent a case. Do not promise to “come back to it” unless you will.

## **When you make a mistake**

Say, “I stand corrected.” Give the right answer quickly and continue. If the mistake changes your submission, fix the submission: “I stand corrected. The correct test is X, and applying it here…” Do not apologize for a long time. Do not argue with the judge.

## **When the judge asks about something you plan to say later**

Do not say, “I will come to that later.” The judge wants the answer now. Better options:

- **Bring it forward.** “I will answer that now by turning to my third submission and then return to my first.”
- **Short answer now, detail later.** “The Respondent’s position is X. I will develop this under my third submission.”

## **When the question is about your teammate’s issue**

Give a short answer if you can. “My co-counsel will address this in detail, but in summary, the Applicant’s position is…” If you do not know, say so and point to your co-counsel.

## **When the judge helps you**

Sometimes a judge suggests a better argument for you. Take it if it is good and build on it so it shows you understand it. But be careful: do not accept an argument that goes further than your case needs, or that opens a new weakness. You may say, “If the Court accepts that, it supports the Applicant. The Applicant’s primary submission is a narrower one, which is…”

## **When to concede**

Do not defend a point that cannot be won if conceding it costs you nothing. A calm concession builds trust.

**Formula:**

```
We accept X. But X does not change the result because Y.
```

Never concede the heart of your case.

## **When the judge is hostile or silent**

- **Hostile or fast questions.** Stay calm. Say “respectfully”. Answer one question at a time. Keep your voice steady. Your frustration should not be visible in your face, voice or demeanor.
- **Silent bench.** Do not panic. Keep explaining. Use your own signposts and ask, “Would the Court like me to expand on this point?” only if it helps.
- **A judge cuts you off to change the order.** Follow the judge’s order. Their order is the right order.

## **Do not do these**

- Do not interrupt the judge, even to “save time”.
- Do not say “That is a good question”.
- Do not give long speeches. Answers should be short and direct.
- Do not repeat your whole argument in each answer.
- Do not read your notes while answering. Look at the judge.
- Do not guess a case name or a number. Say you cannot be sure.

## **Practice drill: the 30-second answer**

Write 20 hard questions. For each, answer in 30 seconds or less using the five-step answer. Record yourself. Listen back. Does the answer begin with the answer? Does it include one source? Does it return to your case? Repeat until all 20 are smooth.
